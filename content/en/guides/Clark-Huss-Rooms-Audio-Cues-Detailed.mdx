Audio cues in Clark Huss rooms are not just sound effects. They are the primary information channel that the game uses to communicate Clark's position, alertness state, and behavioral pattern. Every veteran player you see clearing runs is interpreting these cues in real time without conscious effort. New players hear the same sounds but cannot translate them into actionable information, which is the gap that keeps them dying early.

This guide provides a complete reference for every audio cue in the game, the exact conditions that trigger each one, and the actionable response for each cue. You will also learn how to distinguish Clark audio from environmental audio and how audio cues change as the run progresses.

## The Audio Cue Taxonomy

Clark Huss rooms uses roughly twenty distinct audio cues that fall into four categories. Understanding the categories helps you prioritize listening attention.

### **Audio cue categories:**

| Category | Cue Count | Priority |
|----------|-----------|----------|
| Clark entity audio | 6 | Highest |
| Environmental audio | 7 | Medium |
| Player-generated audio | 5 | Lowest |
| Server state audio | 2 | Special |

Player-generated audio is your own footsteps, flashlight clicks, and collisions. You should hear your own audio at low volume because it helps you maintain discipline. Server state audio includes the lobby ambient and the match timer.

## Clark Entity Audio Cues

Clark produces six distinct audio cues that communicate his position, alertness, and behavioral state. Each cue has a specific trigger condition and actionable response.

### **The six Clark audio cues:**

| Cue | Trigger Condition | Sound Characteristics | Distance Indicator | Response |
|-----|-------------------|----------------------|---------------------|----------|
| Heartbeat | Clark is 2+ corridors away, unaware | Low pulse, regular rhythm | Volume scales with proximity | Continue current pace |
| Breathing | Clark is 1 corridor away, aware | Rhythmic exhale, deeper pitch than player breathing | Medium volume | Pause, assess direction |
| Footstep thuds | Clark is in adjacent room | Heavy, slow, distinct from player footsteps | Loud | Move to escape route |
| Whisper fragments | Clark has high alertness | Soft, distorted words, intermittent | Variable | Prepare for chase |
| Sprint burst | Clark is in active chase | Loud rapid footsteps, sudden onset | Very loud | Full sprint, flashlight off |
| Vocalization | Clark is in detection range | Single-word mutter, rare | Close | Immediate evasion |

The six cues form a progression from distant (heartbeat) to immediate (vocalization). Veterans learn to identify the cue within one second of hearing it.

## Heartbeat Cue Analysis

The heartbeat is Clark's most common cue because it plays continuously when he is in the server. The heartbeat's rhythm and volume communicate his alertness state.

### **Heartbeat variations:**

| Variation | BPM Range | Meaning |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| Slow heartbeat | 50 — 60 BPM | Clark patrolling, unaware |
| Normal heartbeat | 60 — 80 BPM | Clark investigating, mildly alert |
| Fast heartbeat | 80 — 100 BPM | Clark tracking, alert |
| Racing heartbeat | 100+ BPM | Clark in pursuit, highly alert |

The BPM is audible to the trained ear, but you can also estimate it by counting beats during a ten-second window and multiplying by six. Veterans count automatically without thinking about it.

## Breathing Cadence Analysis

Clark's breathing is the second most common cue and the most informative. The breathing cadence (rhythm pattern) tells you his alertness state, and the volume tells you distance.

### **Breathing cadence patterns:**

| Pattern | Cadence | Alertness State |
|---------|---------|-----------------|
| Slow exhale | One breath per 4 seconds | Calm patrol |
| Normal exhale | One breath per 2 seconds | Mildly alert |
| Rapid exhale | One breath per second | Active tracking |
| Irregular exhale | Variable | High alertness, possible chase |

The breathing cadence also has a directional component. Because Clark's breathing comes from his position, you can determine his direction by listening for stereo separation in your headphones. Left-channel dominant means Clark is to the left. Right-channel dominant means Clark is to the right.

## Footstep Thud Analysis

Clark's footsteps are distinct from player footsteps in three ways: they are louder, slower, and have a heavier impact sound. The footstep thuds are the primary cue when Clark is in an adjacent room.

**Footstep variations:**

| Variation | Sound | Distance |
|-----------|-------|----------|
| Slow thuds | Two per second | Adjacent room |
| Normal thuds | Three per second | Same corridor, distant |
| Rapid thuds | Four per second | Active chase |
| Sprint thuds | Six per second | Direct pursuit |

The thud pattern changes when Clark's behavioral state changes. A sudden shift from slow thuds to rapid thuds indicates he has detected you and is moving to engage.

## Whisper Fragment Analysis

Whisper fragments are the most distinctive Clark cue because they include actual vocalized words, though distorted. The fragments indicate elevated alertness and are a warning before chase.

**Whisper fragment variations:**

| Fragment Type | Sound | Trigger |
|---------------|-------|---------|
| Single word | "Here..." | Clark has audio confirmation of your position |
| Repeated fragment | "Here... here..." | Clark is tracking your movement |
| Whispered phrase | "Where are you" | Clark is searching based on residual audio |
| Multiple fragments | Rapid succession | Clark has line of sight, chase imminent |

Whisper fragments are rare because they only trigger when Clark is highly alert. New players often miss them because they are soft, but veterans listen specifically for whispers as the prelude to chase.

## Environmental Audio Cues

Environmental audio is not Clark but provides information about the corridor layout, locked doors, and safe zones. Environmental cues are lower priority than Clark cues but still important.

**Environmental audio cues:**

| Cue | Sound | Meaning |
|-----|-------|---------|
| Fluorescent buzz | Constant hum | Standard corridor ambient |
| Door lock click | Sharp click | Locked door nearby |
| Air vent hum | Low rumble | Open room ahead |
| Echo chamber | Long reverb | Large room nearby |
| Dripping water | Intermittent drops | Decoration only |
| Paper rustle | Soft crinkle | Hidden item location |
| Mechanical whir | Sustained mechanical sound | Special event zone |

Environmental audio provides navigation information. New players ignore it, but veterans use it to build their corridor map.

Your own player produces audio that Clark can detect. Understanding your own audio footprint helps you minimize Clark's detection chance.

**Player audio cues:**

| Cue | Sound | Clark Detection |
|-----|-------|-----------------|
| Walking footsteps | Soft, regular | Low detection (5 meters) |

The audio detection ranges are not displayed in-game, but community testing has measured them approximately. Sprinting is the loudest player action and should be reserved for chases only.

**Server state cues:**

Server state cues are the lowest priority but useful for tracking run duration and planning escape timing.

## Directional Audio Localization

Directional audio localization is the skill of identifying which direction a sound comes from. The skill requires stereo headphones and training.

**Localization training sequence:**

1. **Stage One — Stereo Identification:** play a sound and identify whether it is left or right dominant
2. **Stage Two — Front-Back Identification:** play a sound and identify whether it is in front or behind
3. **Stage Three — Distance Estimation:** play a sound and estimate distance in corridor counts
4. **Stage Four — Movement Tracking:** play a moving sound and track its trajectory
5. **Stage Five — Mixed Environment:** play multiple sounds simultaneously and localize each one

Each stage takes about fifteen minutes of focused practice. The full sequence builds localization skills that transfer to gameplay automatically.

The decision tree becomes automatic after ten hours of practice. Veterans execute it without conscious thought.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the most important audio cue to learn first?**

The breathing cadence. It carries distance, direction, and alertness state in a single cue. Master breathing detection before any other cue.

**Can I learn audio cues without playing the game?**

Yes. Community audio training tools isolate cues for practice. You can learn the basic recognition in twenty minutes of tool-based training.

**Do audio cues change between patches?**

Sometimes. During Early Access, the developer may tweak cue volumes or add new variants. Check the patch tracker regularly for audio-related changes.

**What if my headphones have poor stereo separation?**

Even budget stereo headphones provide usable directional audio. If your current setup is mono (single speaker), you cannot play effectively.

**How do I distinguish my own breathing from Clark's?**

Your breathing is a soft inhale-exhale from your own position, heard equally in both ears. Clark's breathing is deeper pitched and comes from a directional source. The volume test is reliable: if you hear it equally in both ears, it is your own breathing.
