Co-op survival in Clark Huss rooms requires coordination that solo play does not demand. The server lock mechanic ensures every squad member is committed from the first door, and the audio-first design rewards squads that communicate effectively. Well-coordinated squads consistently survive two to three times longer than uncoordinated players in the same server.

This guide covers the fundamentals of co-op survival, including squad role assignments, callout systems, formation strategies, and coordinated Clark evasion. You will also learn how to handle squad failures, communication breakdowns, and emergency situations that require team-wide response.

## Why Co-Op Matters

Co-op play fundamentally changes the Clark Huss rooms experience. The differences from solo play extend beyond just having more players in the server.

### **Co-op benefits:**

| Benefit | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| Extended survival time | Coordinated runs last longer |
| Reduced individual pressure | Roles distribute responsibility |
| Audio monitoring shared | Multiple listeners cover more |
| Flashlight battery rotation | Conservation possible |
| Formation escape options | Coordinated retreat |
| Social engagement | Shared experience |

The benefits compound when coordination is high. Top squads achieve survival times that solo players cannot approach.

## The Three Core Roles

Every coordinated squad assigns three roles before the server lock. The roles distribute the cognitive load of navigation, threat detection, and flashlight management.

### **Role definitions:**

| Role | Function | Key Skill |
|------|----------|-----------|
| Lead | Navigation and path selection | Spatial memory |
| Rear Guard | Clark audio monitoring | Audio interpretation |
| Light Manager | Flashlight timing | Click discipline |

In duos, Lead and Light Manager combine. In quatros, Light Manager can split into scanner and clicker sub-roles. In full lobbies, multiple players can share each role.

## Pre-Game Briefing

The pre-game briefing establishes roles and plans before the server lock. Briefings take 30 seconds but prevent most coordination failures.

### **Briefing checklist:**

| Step | Duration |
|------|----------|
| Confirm all players present | 5 sec |
| Assign roles | 10 sec |
| State first-door timing plan | 5 sec |
| Confirm push-to-talk active | 5 sec |
| Acknowledge calm voice designation | 5 sec |

The briefing is brief because extended pre-game discussion often confuses more than it clarifies. Stick to essential points.

## Standard Callout Vocabulary

Standardized callouts let the squad communicate quickly without interpretation overhead. The vocabulary is short and unambiguous.

### **Callout reference:**

| Callout | Meaning |
|---------|---------|
| "Clark left" | Clark is to the left of the speaker |
| "Clark right" | Clark is to the right of the speaker |
| "Clark back" | Clark is behind the squad |
| "Clark front" | Clark is ahead of the squad |
| "Breathing close" | Clark audio tell within one corridor |
| "Chase" | Clark in active pursuit |
| "Clear" | No Clark audio tells in monitored area |
| "Dead end" | No exit, turn around |
| "Door" | Locked door nearby |
| "Junction" | Decision point ahead |
| "Battery half" | Light Manager battery at 50% |
| "Battery critical" | Light Manager battery below 15% |

The vocabulary is shared across the community, so any coordinated squad can communicate with any other.

## Wedge Formation

The wedge formation is the default co-op formation. It maintains visual contact while distributing audio monitoring.

**Wedge formation:**

| Position | Role | Function |
|----------|------|----------|
| Front | Lead | Navigates and decides routes |
| Center | Light Manager | Controls flashlight timing |
| Back | Rear Guard | Monitors for Clark |

The formation can rotate clockwise or counter-clockwise based on squad movement. Rotation prevents role fatigue.

## Single File Formation

The single file formation is used in narrow corridors or when audio monitoring is critical. The formation reduces audio noise from multiple footsteps.

**Single file formation:**

| Position | Role | Function |
|----------|------|----------|
| Front | Lead | Navigates |
| Second | Light Manager | Controls flashlight |
| Third | Rear Guard | Monitors audio |

The single file formation is harder to maintain during chases because players can lose visual contact.

## Buddy System

The buddy system pairs each player with one squadmate. The system prevents isolation, which is Clark's primary targeting mechanism.

**Buddy system rules:**

| Rule | Description |
|------|-------------|
| Each player has one buddy | Pair assignments locked for the run |
| Buddies stay within visual range | Maximum 5 meters apart |
| Buddies cover each other's audio | Each pair monitors two directions |
| If one buddy dies, the survivor joins another pair | Squad reorganizes |

The buddy system is simple but effective at preventing isolation deaths.

## Voice Chat Discipline

Voice chat discipline prevents audio chaos that overwhelms Clark audio tells. The discipline is restrictive but necessary.

**Voice chat rules:**

| Rule | Purpose |
|------|---------|
| Push-to-talk only | Prevents background noise |
| Factual content | Avoids emotional reactions |
| One speaker at a time | Prevents overlapping |
| Brief callouts | Under 3 seconds per callout |
| Calm voice | Measured pace during chases |

Violations of these rules reduce squad coordination effectiveness significantly.

## Emergency Protocols

Emergency protocols trigger specific squad-wide responses. They bypass normal coordination rules because the situation is critical.

**Emergency protocols:**

| Trigger | Signal | Response |
|---------|--------|----------|
| Man down | "Man down, [direction]" | Nearest teammate recovers, others cover |
| Full chase | "Chase, sprint now" | All players sprint, no flashlight |
| Separation | "Separation, [direction]" | Squad pauses, calls out to separated player |
| Battery dead | "Battery dead, [player]" | Squad switches to audio-only |
| Ambush | "Ambush, [direction]" | Immediate retreat, no questions |
| Exit found | "Exit, [direction]" | Squad converges on exit |

These protocols are memorized before the first run. They execute automatically under pressure.

## Light Manager Rotation

The Light Manager role controls flashlight timing for the squad. Rotation among players distributes the battery cost.

**Rotation timing by squad size:**

| Squad Size | Rotation Interval |
|------------|-------------------|
| Duo | 5 minutes |
| Trio | 4 minutes |
| Quatro | 3 minutes |

During rotation, the current Light Manager passes the role to the next player. The rotation is announced with "light passing to [player name]."

## Chase Coordination

During a Clark chase, the squad must respond as a unit. Individual responses lead to isolation and death.

**Chase response protocol:**

1. Rear Guard calls "Chase" the moment active pursuit is detected
2. Squad switches to full sprint, flashlight off
3. Lead chooses escape route with two corner breaks
4. Squad moves as a unit maintaining visual contact
5. Rear Guard monitors for Clark disengagement
6. After disengagement, squad switches to controlled retreat pace

The protocol takes 15 — 20 seconds to execute fully. Discipline during this window determines survival.

## Squad Failure Recovery

When the squad makes mistakes, recovery is possible if the response is immediate. Panic makes recovery harder.

**Common failures and recovery:**

| Failure | Recovery Action |
|---------|-----------------|
| Battery depleted | Switch to audio-only mode |
| Squadmate separated | Pause, call out, regroup |
| Light Manager down | Pass role immediately |
| Lead confused | Nominate new Lead, regroup at junction |
| Multiple chases | Focus on escape, not objectives |
| Voice chat chaos | Designate calm voice, mute others |

Most failures are recoverable if the squad recognizes them early. Recognition requires awareness.

## Role Fatigue Management

Sustained role responsibility causes cognitive fatigue. The squad should rotate roles to prevent errors.

**Fatigue signs:**

| Sign | Action |
|------|--------|
| Missed callouts | Rotate to fresh role |
| Slower response time | Reduce role duration |
| Emotional reactions | Pass role, become Follower |
| Battery depletion | Already rotated out, was Light Manager |
| Decision paralysis | Pass Lead role |

Veteran squads rotate every 3 — 5 minutes to prevent fatigue accumulation.

## Building Squad Cohesion

Squad cohesion comes from consistent play with the same squadmates. Random public lobbies rarely achieve cohesion.

**Cohesion building strategies:**

| Strategy | Effect |
|----------|--------|
| Play with same squad regularly | Builds coordination habits |
| Review failed runs together | Identifies coordination breakdowns |
| Practice in private lobbies | Refines role assignment |
| Use community Discord | Finds compatible squadmates |
| Set squad standards | Maintains quality over time |

The best squads have 3 — 5 players who play together weekly. Coordination becomes automatic.

- Pre-agree on emergency protocols for chase and battery situations

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the ideal squad size for Clark Huss rooms?**

Three players is ideal. The trio provides role coverage without coordination overhead becoming unmanageable.

**How do I find good squadmates?**

Community Discord and Wiki squad recruitment are the best sources. Consistent play with the same squadmates is the fastest path to good coordination.

**Can I play with random public players?**

Yes, but coordination will be limited. Public lobby coordination requires explicit pre-game agreement, which most random players do not engage with.

**How long does it take to build squad cohesion?**

Typically 5 — 10 sessions with the same squadmates. After 20 sessions, coordination becomes automatic.

**What if my squad cannot agree on roles?**

Assign roles based on each player's strengths. Audio-savvy players become Rear Guard. Spatial-thinky players become Lead. Disciplined players become Light Manager.

For more on the role mechanics in detail, see our [multiplayer coordination guide](/en/guides/multiplayer-coordination/). To learn the escape tactics that require coordination, read [escaping Clark](/en/guides/escaping-clark/).


**How do I handle toxic squadmates?**

Mute toxic players immediately. Focus on coordinating with positive squadmates. If the entire squad is toxic, leave the lobby and find better players. Long-term squad building through community channels reduces exposure to random toxic behavior.

**What if my squad disagrees on strategy?**

Pre-game strategy agreement prevents mid-run conflicts. If disagreements arise during play, defer to the assigned Lead. Post-run discussions can address strategy issues constructively. Avoid changing strategies mid-run unless facing immediate failure.

**How do I improve as a squad over time?**

Regular play together builds coordination habits. Review failed runs to identify improvement areas. Practice specific scenarios like chases or battery management. Rotate roles to develop well-rounded squad members. Track progress over multiple sessions.

**Should I use Roblox voice chat or Discord?**

Discord generally provides better audio quality and more features than Roblox voice chat. Discord supports push-to-talk, noise suppression, and channel separation. Roblox voice is convenient but less reliable. Most coordinated squads prefer Discord.

**What is the best way to communicate in chaotic lobbies?**

Use standardized callouts and avoid extended discussions. Push-to-talk discipline prevents audio clutter. Calm voice designation helps stabilize communication. Mute chaotic players to focus on your squad.

**Can I play with friends across different time zones?**

Time zone differences require scheduling flexibility. Some squads coordinate across multiple time zones by playing at overlapping times. Others form regional sub-squads. Find what works for your specific situation.

**How do I build trust with new squadmates?**

Trust builds through consistent play and reliable performance. Show up when scheduled, perform your role, and communicate clearly. Avoid drama and focus on gameplay. Trust develops naturally over multiple sessions.
