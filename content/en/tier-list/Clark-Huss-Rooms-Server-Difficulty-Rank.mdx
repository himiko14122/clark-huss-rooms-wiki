Clark Huss rooms runs on 50-player servers, but the actual gameplay experience varies dramatically based on how many of those players are coordinating together. Solo players in a 50-player server face a completely different challenge than coordinated trios in the same server. Understanding the difficulty differences between server configurations helps you choose servers that match your skill level and squad composition.

This tier list ranks server configurations from S (most favorable) to D (most challenging), explains the variables that affect difficulty, and provides recommendations for optimal server selection. You will also learn how player skill distribution affects server difficulty, and the matchmaking factors that influence your server assignment.

## Server Configuration Tier Framework

Each server configuration is rated based on three factors: coordination potential, Clark encounter frequency, and resource availability. The factors determine the difficulty and viability of different server types.

### **Server configuration factors:**

| Factor | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| Coordination potential | How well players can work together |
| Clark encounter frequency | How often Clark targets the squad |
| Resource availability | Flashlight battery, stamina, space |

The combination produces a tier ranking from S (easiest to succeed) to D (hardest to survive).

## Tier S: Coordinated Trio Server

Coordinated trios in partially full servers represent the optimal configuration because the squad has enough role coverage without coordination overhead becoming unmanageable.

### **Tier S configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | 10 — 25 active (squad size 3) |
| Coordination quality | High (consistent squadmates) |
| Clark encounter rate | Moderate |
| Battery management | Excellent (3-player rotation) |
| Survival time average | 8 — 12 minutes |

The trio configuration allows Lead, Rear Guard, and Light Manager role assignment without overcommitting any single player. The squad can sustain flashlight usage through rotation and maintain formation throughout the run.

## Tier A: Coordinated Quatro Server

Coordinated quatros offer more role specialization than trios but introduce additional coordination overhead. The configuration works well for experienced squads.

### **Tier A configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | 15 — 30 active (squad size 4) |
| Coordination quality | High (consistent squadmates) |
| Clark encounter rate | Higher than trio (more audio noise) |
| Battery management | Very good (4-player rotation) |
| Survival time average | 7 — 11 minutes |

The quatro has an extra player to cover specialized roles (scanner, callout monitor, escape route planner), but the additional audio noise from four players increases Clark's detection chance.

## Tier B: Coordinated Duo Server

Coordinated duos work well but lack role specialization. Both players must cover multiple roles, which increases cognitive load.

### **Tier B configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | 8 — 20 active (squad size 2) |
| Coordination quality | High (consistent squadmates) |
| Clark encounter rate | Low (only 2 audio sources) |
| Battery management | Limited (2-player rotation) |
| Survival time average | 6 — 10 minutes |

The duo's main advantage is low audio footprint. Two players produce minimal noise, which makes Clark encounters less frequent. The disadvantage is that battery management has less flexibility.

## Tier B: Solo in Partial Server

Solo play in a partial server (10 — 30 players) offers a unique experience because you can navigate without coordinating with anyone while still benefiting from the ambient player activity.

**Tier B solo configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | 10 — 30 active, solo player |
| Coordination quality | N/A (solo) |
| Clark encounter rate | Low |
| Battery management | Individual |
| Survival time average | 5 — 9 minutes |

The solo in partial server configuration works for players who want to practice audio skills without coordination overhead. The lack of squad means no role distribution, but also no coordination complexity.

## Tier C: Full Lobby (50 Players)

A full 50-player server is the worst configuration for coordinated play because too many audio sources compete for Clark's attention and squelch coordinated movement.

**Tier C full lobby configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | 50 active |
| Coordination quality | Poor (random public) |
| Clark encounter rate | High |
| Battery management | Competing |
| Survival time average | 2 — 5 minutes |

The full lobby is chaotic because most players are not coordinating. Your squad is competing with 47 other players for Clark's attention, flashlight usage, and route options.

## Tier C: Solo in Full Lobby

Solo play in a full lobby combines the worst aspects of solo play (no coordination) with the worst aspects of full lobbies (maximum chaos). New players often default to this configuration.

**Tier C solo full lobby configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | 50 active, solo player |
| Coordination quality | None |
| Clark encounter rate | Very high |
| Battery management | Individual, competing |
| Survival time average | 1 — 4 minutes |

Most deaths in Clark Huss rooms happen in this configuration because new players do not understand the dynamics. They join a full lobby solo, panic when Clark approaches, and sprint to their death.

## Tier D: Public Lobby With Strangers

Joining a public lobby with strangers for a "spontaneous squad" is the lowest-ranked configuration because it combines all the negatives: high player count, low coordination, and inconsistent player behavior.

**Tier D public lobby configuration:**

| Aspect | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Player count | Variable (often full) |
| Coordination quality | Very poor |
| Clark encounter rate | Very high |
| Battery management | Chaotic |
| Survival time average | 30 sec — 3 minutes |

The public lobby configuration is a learning environment, not a survival strategy. New players should treat each public lobby run as practice, not as a serious survival attempt.

## Player Skill Distribution Effect

Skill distribution within a server affects difficulty because Clark's targeting behavior is influenced by the variance in player behavior, not just the count.

**Skill distribution impact:**

| Skill Distribution | Difficulty Impact |
|--------------------|-------------------|
| All experienced | Lower difficulty (coordinated, predictable) |
| Mixed skilled | Medium difficulty (unpredictable behavior) |
| Mostly new | Higher difficulty (panic behavior common) |
| All new | Highest difficulty (chaos) |

The skill distribution is not directly controllable, but it correlates with server freshness. New servers tend to have new players; established servers tend to have veterans.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the best server configuration for a new player?**

Solo in a partial server with 10 — 30 players. The configuration provides practice without overwhelming chaos.

**Should I always play with a coordinated squad?**

Yes, for serious survival attempts. Random public lobbies are practice environments only.

**Is a full 50-player server always bad?**

Not necessarily. Some full lobbies have coordinated squads that perform well. The challenge is identifying whether the squad is coordinated before committing.

**How do I find coordinated squadmates?**

Community Discord and the Wiki squad recruitment section are the best sources. Many veterans form squads through these channels.

**What is the maximum recommended squad size?**

Quatro (4 players) is the maximum recommended size for coordinated play. Larger squads introduce coordination overhead that exceeds the benefit.

For more on the coordination protocols that make coordinated squads effective, see our [co-op survival guide](/en/multiplayer/co-op-survival-guide/). To learn the strategies that work best in solo play, read [beginner guide](/en/guides/beginner-guide/).


**How often should I re-evaluate tier rankings?**

Tier rankings should be re-evaluated after major patches and quarterly during stable periods. Strategy effectiveness shifts with game changes. Veterans track tier rankings over time and adjust their playstyle accordingly. Personal tier rankings may differ from community consensus.

**Are tier rankings universal or platform-specific?**

Tier rankings have platform-specific variations. PC players have advantages in audio-dependent strategies. Mobile players may prefer different approaches due to control and audio quality differences. Consider platform when applying tier rankings to your play.

**How do I create my own tier rankings?**

Track your own performance with different strategies across multiple runs. Note success rates, survival times, and enjoyment. Combine personal data with community consensus to form your own rankings. Update rankings as you gain more experience and the game evolves.

**Should tier rankings influence my strategy choice?**

Tier rankings inform strategy choice but should not dictate it. Personal preference and playstyle matter alongside effectiveness. A slightly weaker strategy that matches your strengths may outperform a stronger strategy that does not fit your playstyle. Balance rankings with personal factors.

**What is the difference between tier rankings and meta?**

Tier rankings evaluate individual strategies in isolation. Meta describes the overall strategic landscape including dominant combinations, counter-strategies, and balance shifts. Both are useful for understanding the game state. Meta analysis often references tier rankings as components.

**How do squad strategies affect tier rankings?**

Squad strategies often rank higher than solo strategies because coordination multiplies effectiveness. Solo strategies rank higher in solo play contexts. When evaluating tier rankings, consider whether the strategy is intended for solo or squad play. Some strategies only work in one context.

**Can tier rankings be wrong?**

Tier rankings are community consensus based on observation, not absolute truth. Individual experiences may differ from consensus. Some strategies that rank lower may work better for specific players. Use tier rankings as guidance, not absolute rules.
